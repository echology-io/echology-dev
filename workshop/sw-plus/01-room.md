# 01 — The room

Everything below the role line is inference from the roles and background given in
the brief. Treat it as a hypothesis to test in the first thirty minutes, not as
knowledge. Where the room contradicts a card, the room is right — mark the card in
`04-capture.md` and move.

---

## William O'Neal — Vice President

**Lane:** executive. Probably the person whose "yes" ends the budget conversation,
and the person most likely to leave early if the session drifts technical.

Measuring: growth, profitability, delivery, utilization, risk, differentiation,
recruiting, and what breaks when the firm scales.

**First question to him, by name:** *"What differentiates your best offices from your
average ones? Not the numbers — the thing underneath the numbers."*

That question does three jobs. It flatters nothing. It assumes he has thought about
it, which he has. And the answer is almost always *a person* or *a way of working
that never got written down*, which is the exact center of the session.

**Leans in on:** anything about scale breaking things. What becomes impossible at 2x
headcount. Why the best office is best. Retention of knowledge when senior people
retire — at a firm with 23-year tenures, someone is retiring.

**You lose him with:** architecture, model names, anything he cannot repeat to a
board or a partner in one sentence.

**What he should be able to say afterward, unprompted:** one sentence about
capability, not about a tool. If he says "so it is a way for us to keep what our
senior people know" you have won the executive lane outright.

## David Richter — Director of IT, 23 years at SW+

**Lane:** IT, and separately: institutional memory in human form.

Twenty-three years means he has personally survived every technology direction the
firm has taken, including the ones that did not work. He has watched vendors arrive
with certainty and leave. His skepticism, if present, is earned and is not personal.

Likely owns: enterprise systems, technology direction, infrastructure, long-term
governance. If it will still be running in five years, he owns it.

**First question to him, by name:** *"Twenty-three years — you have seen this firm
change shape more than once. What is the thing about how SW+ works that has never
changed, no matter what we put in front of it?"*

This is the single highest-value question available in the room and it is his alone
to answer. It positions him as the source of institutional truth rather than as a
gatekeeper to route around, and the answer is a direct read on what any pilot has to
survive contact with.

**Leans in on:** longevity, ownership, what happens when the vendor disappears, data
custody, integration with what already exists rather than replacement of it.

**You lose him with:** anything that implies the current stack is a problem to be
solved. He built it. Also: any answer implying the firm should change its process to
fit a tool.

**Watch for:** he may be quiet. Quiet from a 23-year IT director is not agreement, it
is evaluation. If he has not spoken by T+45, ask him the question above directly.

## Robert Nettles — IT Operations & Security Manager

**Lane:** IT. Zero Trust, identity, NIST, CIS, governance, Microsoft ecosystem,
infrastructure, operational resilience.

This is the most technically aligned person in the room with respect to what has
actually been built, and he may not realize it for the first hour.

Someone who thinks in NIST and CIS terms does not evaluate AI on capability. He
evaluates it on whether a claim can be substantiated, and he has almost certainly
been in meetings this year where an AI vendor could not substantiate one. That
experience is your opening, and it is why the provenance line lands with him and
nobody else in the room.

**First question to him, by name:** *"What concerns you most about AI here? Not the
headline risks — the specific thing you would have to answer for."*

**The one line, if and only if governance comes up:**

> "AI should not be trusted because AI said something. It should be trusted because
> every conclusion can be traced back, independently, to observed evidence."

**Then stop.** Do not explain provenance further. Do not say the words "signal
provenance" unless he asks what to call it. He will finish the thought himself, and a
conclusion he completes is worth ten you deliver. If he asks a follow-up, answer that
question only.

**Leans in on:** evidence, traceability, the ability to audit a conclusion, the honest
"we tested this and it did not work" (see the material in `02-run-of-show.md` Part 5).

**You lose him with:** confident claims with nothing behind them. One unsubstantiated
number and he will correctly discount everything that follows it. Never state a
figure in this room you cannot source on the spot.

**Likely champion for governance.** If the pilot has a governance shape, he is the
one who makes it survivable internally.

## Landen Wilson — IT Coordinator

**Lane:** IT by title, practice by temperament. Cybersecurity, Python, former Navy.

Most likely person in the room to go build something the same week. Navy background
suggests process discipline and comfort with a chain of custody; Python plus security
suggests he is already automating things nobody has asked him to automate.

**First question to him, by name:** *"What have you already automated here that
nobody asked you to?"*

The answer is a free map of where the friction actually is, delivered by someone with
no incentive to editorialize. Ask it in Part 3 and let it run.

**Leans in on:** APIs, plugins, integration surfaces, whether he can drive it himself.

**You lose him with:** anything that looks like a closed system he would have to file
a ticket against.

**Potential future champion.** Not the decision maker, and that is precisely why he
matters: he is the one who will still be trying it in three weeks when everyone else
has moved on. If exactly one person leaves with something to try, make it him.

## York Dilday — Civil Digital Practice Lead

**Lane:** practice. Civil3D, CAD management, automation, AutoLISP, C#, workflow
optimization, mentorship.

Someone writing AutoLISP and C# against Civil3D has already built the primitive
version of what you would propose, in a language most people consider legacy, because
the need was real enough to justify it. Respect that investment out loud and early.

**First question to him, by name:** *"What have you written in LISP or C# that you
wish you had not had to write? Something the platform should have done for you."*

**Leans in on:** meeting existing tooling where it is rather than replacing it,
extending what he has already built, the gap between what the standard says and what
the drawing does.

**You lose him with:** any suggestion that his automation is technical debt to be
replaced. It is not debt. It is the evidence that the pain is real and has been real
for years.

## Christopher Grodde — Civil3D Practice Leader, Senior PM

**Lane:** practice, plus delivery. Mentorship, training, internal standards.

Publicly commented that he wants to test plugins. That is a stated intention to
adopt, on the record, before the meeting started.

**First question to him, by name:** *"You said you wanted to test plugins — what were
you hoping one would do?"*

Quoting him back to himself, accurately, in front of his colleagues, does more for
credibility than any slide. It also produces a pilot candidate in the first sixty
seconds of the practice lane.

**Leans in on:** anything he can try. Concrete over conceptual, every time.

**Likely early adopter and likely the loudest voice for participation in Part 4.**
Use him to open the thought experiment if the room is slow to start — he will answer
first and give everyone else permission.

**Careful:** an early adopter's enthusiasm can pull the session into a feature
conversation an hour too early. If he starts specifying, park it visibly: *"That is a
pilot candidate — I am writing it on the board so we do not lose it"* — then return
to the map.

## Jacob Esposito — Civil Digital Practice Lead, Charlotte

**Lane:** practice, plus the multi-office question. Configuration, Civil3D
environments, roadway, standards, automation.

He is in a different office than the rest of the room. That is not a logistical
detail, it is the most useful natural experiment available: two offices, same firm,
same standards on paper, different reality in practice.

**First question to him, by name:** *"Where does Charlotte do it differently from
here? Not better or worse — differently."*

Ask it in front of everyone. The divergence between offices is exactly what the VP
means when he talks about what differentiates the best offices, and Jacob is the only
person who can answer it from inside both.

**Leans in on:** standards that hold up across environments, configuration drift,
onboarding someone new to a project in a different office.

## Also in the room

Landscape Architecture digital practice, civil PMs, CAD leaders, other internal
technical leadership.

Do not let the named list crowd them out. The unnamed attendees are less likely to
have a position to defend and more likely to say the honest thing about daily work.

**Around T+35, before Part 3, ask the room generally:** *"Who here has not said
anything yet, and is currently disagreeing with something on this board?"* Asked
lightly, it works, and it usually produces the most useful sentence of the hour.

## Reading the room live

| Signal | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two people give different answers to the same process question | Process divergence they may not know about | Draw both on the board. Ask which is the standard. The silence that follows is data. |
| Someone describes a workaround with pride | Real pain, already absorbed and normalized | Ask how long they have done it that way. Long-lived workarounds are the best pilot candidates. |
| An exec asks a technical question | They are trying to gauge whether it is real | Answer briefly, then hand it to IT to evaluate in front of them. Their trust in their own people transfers to you. |
| IT asks how it fails | Serious evaluation, not resistance | Answer with a real failure. This is the best moment in the session. Do not waste it on reassurance. |
| Room goes quiet after a question | Either the question landed or it missed | Count to five. Do not rescue it. Five seconds of silence is where the honest answer comes from. |
| Someone says "well, in theory..." | The standard and the practice have diverged | *"And in practice?"* Nothing else. |
