## 3. Hidden organizational patterns

The things nobody said directly, that the transcript implies. Support each with at
least two independent observations; a pattern with one observation is a hunch, label
it as such.

| Pattern | Evidence (two or more, attributed) | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  |  |  |
|  |  |  |

Look specifically for:

- **Divergence they do not know about** — two people describing the same process
  differently. Highest-value finding available from a session like this, because
  they cannot see it from inside and you saw it in one afternoon.
- **The delay chain** — where delay is felt vs. where it originates. If they are
  different places, the firm is optimizing the wrong end.
- **The single point of institutional failure** — one person, named repeatedly, who
  is the reason something works. Count how many times each name was invoked by
  someone other than themselves.
- **Standards gap** — the difference between what the standard says and what the
  drawing does, and whether it is knowledge, findability, or disagreement.
- **Office asymmetry** — Charlotte vs. the rest. Which is the exception?
- **Records of *why*** — if decisions are traceable to *what* but never *why*, that
  is the whole opportunity in one sentence and it should be quoted from them.
- **The invisible organization** — everything happening before the system of record
  starts. If the region left of the first box turned out to be large, that is the
  finding of the session: the firm's tools begin measuring after the most consequential
  decisions have already been made, by the people it can least afford to lose.
- **Energy vs. magnitude** — where the room got loud versus where the arithmetic says
  the problem is. If those are different places, say so explicitly and pick energy for
  the first pilot. Magnitude wins arguments; energy survives budget season.

## 5. Pilot candidates

One block per candidate. Two or three, not more. Score honestly — a candidate that
fails a feasibility field is a candidate that dies in month two, and it is much
cheaper to notice that now.

**Selection rule:** rank by energy first, feasibility second, magnitude third.

The highest-energy topic is the one the room will still be talking about in three
weeks, and it is the one that gets defended internally when you are not in the
building. A larger problem that scored 1 or 2 on the gauge is a problem the
organization has already made peace with; a pilot aimed at it has to generate its own
sponsorship, which no pilot has ever done. If the highest-energy topic is not
feasible for a first pilot, say so plainly in the proposal and name it as the second
one — do not silently substitute the safe candidate and hope nobody notices which
conversation it came from.

## 8. What goes back to them, and when

**Within 48 hours — the map.** Their drawing, transcribed, in their words, with the
disagreements shown rather than resolved. No proposal attached. No pricing. It is a
gift and it should read like one.

Attach one sentence: *"If I got anything wrong, tell me — if the map is wrong, the
rest will be too."* An invitation to correct you is an invitation to keep talking,
and a correction is a second conversation you did not have to ask for.

**Within a week — the pilot proposal**, only if a candidate emerged from the room.
If none did, do not manufacture one; send the map and ask for the two follow-up
conversations instead. A pilot invented in the office after the fact will read as a
pilot invented in the office after the fact.

**Held back for now:** anything about scope beyond the first pilot, anything about
platform, anything about cost past the immediate next step.
